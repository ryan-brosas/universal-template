# Messaging, Positioning, and Awareness

Load this capsule for segmentation decisions, One Reader selection, positioning,
category framing, value propositions, awareness, sophistication, message
hierarchy, or multi-audience routing. Load `research-voc.md` first when accepted
customer evidence and a message bank do not exist.

This capsule decides the canonical meaning. Channel capsules decide section
order, copy expression, and interface placement. Frameworks organize the final
argument; validation tests it.

## Input contract

Start with accepted inputs or label their absence:

- audience evidence and business priority;
- triggering situations, desired outcomes, anxieties, and purchase criteria;
- current alternatives, workarounds, substitutes, and status quo;
- product capabilities, mechanism, limits, price, and offer;
- verified claims and proof inventory;
- entry routes, prior exposure, and known awareness;
- competitor category labels, promises, mechanisms, and proof.

Label every strategic input as:

- **observed:** the route or accepted evidence states it;
- **inferred:** several accepted facts strongly support it;
- **assumed:** evidence is absent or ambiguous.

Do not turn an assumption into an approved claim. If a missing input could
change the chosen reader, category, dominant value, or promise, keep the output
in draft and request the smallest resolving evidence.

## Build the decision-context segment map

Segment by differences that change the decision case, not by demographics
alone.

| Field | Decision it captures |
|---|---|
| Actor | person making, using, approving, or influencing the choice |
| Situation or trigger | concrete episode that makes the offer relevant now |
| Job or want | progress the actor is trying to make |
| Recognized problem | symptom or obstacle the actor can already name |
| Believed cause | what the actor thinks creates the problem |
| Actual alternative | competitor, workaround, DIY behavior, delay, or status quo |
| Category fluency | how the actor names available approaches |
| Awareness | what the entry route already knows about problem, solution, product, offer |
| Premium value | the value that wins the relevant tradeoff |
| Next decision | choice the actor must make at this touchpoint |
| Blocking objection | concern that could change that choice |

Demographics matter only when they alter one of these fields. “Small business
owners” is not a useful segment when owners in the same size band face different
triggers, alternatives, values, or decisions.

### Merge or split

Merge candidate segments when they share:

- the same job and triggering situation;
- the same actual alternative;
- one dominant value;
- the same category frame and lead requirement;
- materially similar objections and next decision.

Split them when any of those changes the promise, proof, lead, offer, or action.
If only examples, terminology, or proof emphasis differs, retain one route and
adapt a support module. Do not create routes for superficial personalization.

## Select One Reader

One Reader means one actor in one decision episode. It is not the entire market,
a composite committee, or a fictional persona biography.

A candidate passes only when:

1. The offer can truthfully help in this situation.
2. Accepted evidence supports the job, problem, or want.
3. The actor has an identifiable actual alternative.
4. One value can be made primary.
5. The next decision is clear.
6. The business brief identifies the segment as a priority.
7. Available proof can support the intended promise.

Tie-break in this order:

1. explicit commercial priority;
2. stronger offer fit and substantiation;
3. more coherent decision case;
4. fewer conflicting awareness or category assumptions.

When candidates still require different leads, promises, or offers, choose one
or create separate routes. Never average them into a reader who exists only on
paper.

### Buying groups

Choose the actor whose next decision the asset should change. Then support other
stakeholders without replacing the primary reader:

| Stakeholder | Typical decision question | Messaging role |
|---|---|---|
| User | Will this improve the work? | workflow, outcome, adoption effort |
| Champion | Can I justify and spread this? | internal case, proof, implementation |
| Economic buyer | Is the value worth the cost and risk? | business impact, terms, risk |
| Technical or security reviewer | Will this meet requirements? | architecture, controls, evidence |
| Procurement or legal | Can we contract safely? | policy, terms, documentation |

If stakeholders disagree about the category, value, or offer, create supporting
routes or assets rather than stacking every concern into the primary message.

## Map the market and actual alternatives

Audit about three relevant competitors and the non-product alternatives found in
research. Capture:

| Alternative | Audience | Category | Main promise | Mechanism | Proof | Tradeoff | Repeated language |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Include spreadsheets, email, manual work, agencies, hiring, delay, and “do
nothing” when those are what customers cross-shop in practice. A competitor-only
audit can miss the strongest position because the real rival may be a text
message or an entrenched workflow.

Use the audit to find:

- promises everyone makes;
- differences customers cannot detect;
- category expectations that must be met;
- unclaimed but desirable value;
- tradeoffs competitors avoid naming;
- proof standards the market has trained buyers to expect.

Do not borrow competitor wording or assume an unclaimed space is valuable.
Customer evidence must connect the opening to the decision.

## Decide the category frame

The category helps the reader classify the offer and choose relevant
alternatives. Use the lowest-complexity truthful frame that preserves the
meaningful difference.

| Reader and market condition | Default frame | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Familiar category accurately sets expectations | use the familiar category | do not invent a new label |
| Accurate category is commoditized | add one audience, use-case, or mechanism qualifier | qualifier must affect choice |
| Novel workflow has an accurate familiar analogy | use the bridge, then state the departure | analogy must not erase the difference |
| Existing labels imply the wrong buyer, job, or outcome | consider a subcategory or new frame | document the recurring misclassification |
| Reader knows the want or problem, not solution categories | use a plain descriptor; lead with the want or problem | do not force category jargon into the lead |
| Evidence is insufficient | retain a neutral description | mark the decision assumed |

A new category is justified only when familiar labels repeatedly create material
misunderstanding and the team can define the new frame plainly. Novel naming is
not positioning by itself. Unknown brands generally need more classification
help than established or referred brands.

Before finalizing, complete internally:

- “Readers should classify this as ...”
- “The alternatives they will compare are ...”
- “This frame is useful because ...”
- “This frame could mislead them by ...”

## Find a meaningful differentiator

Generate candidate differences from:

- product design, workflow, or mechanism;
- specific audience, situation, or use case;
- business model, operating policy, or point of view;
- founder, team, or methodology provenance;
- service, support, implementation, or customer focus;
- outcome, tradeoff, or constraint the offer handles differently.

A candidate must be:

- **desirable:** it helps One Reader choose or progress;
- **specific:** the reader can understand what differs;
- **defensible:** product facts and proof support it;
- **relevant:** it matters against the actual alternative;
- **durable enough:** it is not merely a temporary wording gap;
- **compatible:** the offer can deliver it without hiding a major tradeoff.

Use the substitution test: if a credible competitor can replace the product name
and leave the statement materially true, the candidate is category copy, not a
position. Category copy may still orient; it cannot carry differentiation.

Use the tradeoff test: ask what the product chooses to optimize, whom that helps,
and what it does not prioritize. A bounded position is often more credible than
unsupported superiority.

If no desirable and supportable difference survives, stop. Report an offer,
product, or evidence gap instead of wordsmithing “better,” “easy,” “all-in-one,”
or “AI-powered.”

## Write the positioning decision

Complete these internal statements first:

```text
The actual alternative is ...
Readers should classify this as ...
This is the one that ...
The value that wins the decision is ...
The reason to believe is ...
The fit boundary or tradeoff is ...
```

Then summarize:

> For **[One Reader in a situation]**, **[product]** is the **[category or useful
> frame]** that **[distinct valuable outcome]**, because **[credible mechanism or
> reason to believe]**. Unlike **[actual alternative]**, it **[meaningful
> contrast]**.

Treat this as a strategy record, not final copy. Check:

- Does One Reader recognize the situation?
- Does the category orient without flattening the difference?
- Is one value clearly primary?
- Is the contrast meaningful against the actual alternative?
- Does the mechanism make the promise plausible?
- Is proof available at the strength the promise needs?
- Are fit boundaries and tradeoffs honest?

## Diagnose awareness, familiarity, and sophistication

Keep three dimensions separate:

| Dimension | Unit | Changes |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | reader plus entry route | lead and unresolved information needs |
| Brand familiarity | reader plus entry route | amount of product orientation required |
| Market sophistication | segment and category | specificity, mechanism, proof, and contrast |

A mature market can contain unaware readers. A familiar brand can launch a novel
product. Do not use one label to stand in for all three.

### Awareness diagnosis

| Evidence about the route | State | Lead with | Do not assume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recognizes a situation or symptom but cannot name a problem | Unaware | recognizable situation, symptom, or tension | hidden cause or solution interest |
| Names the problem but not its extent, cause, or solution types | Problem-aware | specific problem, stakes, and desired change | category fluency |
| Names approaches or category, not this product | Solution-aware | outcome, approach, mechanism, and contrast | product credibility |
| Knows the product and compares fit or belief | Product-aware | product, differentiator, proof, objections, fit | offer readiness |
| Knows product and value and decides terms or action | Most aware | offer, terms, real constraint, next step | need for category education |

Use a want-led opening when the route clearly knows the desired result but not
the product. Use a product-led opening when the product itself is known. Use a
problem-led opening when the recognized problem must become a specific need.

For problem-aware readers, distinguish:

- symptom they recognize;
- severity they accept;
- cause they believe;
- cause the evidence supports;
- workarounds already tried;
- desired change they can name.

Do not invent hidden emotional stakes or tell readers they misunderstand their
problem without evidence. Mark the awareness diagnosis observed, inferred, or
assumed. Mixed states that require different leads need separate routes or an
entry choice; they do not need a universal opener.

### Brand familiarity

For an unknown brand, answer category, audience, value, and credibility earlier.
For a known or referred brand, preserve orientation but move sooner to the
specific product, difference, fit, or offer. Familiarity can reduce explanation;
it does not reduce the need for proof of a new claim.

### Sophistication response

Use the lowest response that creates a credible distinction:

| Observed market pattern | Response | Reject |
|---|---|---|
| Competitors do not state the outcome clearly | state a direct, specific outcome | unnecessary category invention |
| Competitors repeat broad claims | narrow audience, situation, or outcome | a louder version of the same claim |
| Competitors repeat the same outcome | explain a credible mechanism or workflow | invented mechanism language |
| Competitors repeat the same mechanism | emphasize proof, design, service, point of view, or tradeoff | unsupported superiority |
| Category language is inflated or distrusted | use bounded claims, fit conditions, and concrete support | claim escalation |

Sophistication is observed market sameness, not a license to become dramatic.
If no credible next response exists, report a positioning gap.

## Choose the dominant value

Generic bundles such as “save time and money” avoid the strategic choice. Ask:

1. What resource, outcome, identity, or capability is at a premium in this
   decision?
2. Which value wins when the reader must trade one for another?
3. What does saved time or money enable?
4. What may outrank both, such as control, confidence, speed to a deadline,
   status, safety, autonomy, or reduced risk?
5. Which value is evidenced in actual selection and objection language?

Force-rank evidence-backed values. Select one primary value; treat the others as
supporting consequences or proof. The dominant value may differ by route. Do not
infer a private motive merely because it would make stronger copy.

## Develop the value proposition

A value proposition is the compact decision case, often expressed through a
headline, subhead, and supporting points. It is not a slogan, mission, company
description, feature inventory, benefits pile, or unsupported superlative.

### Generate strategic candidates

First complete:

> My product is the one that **[specific desirable difference]**.

Then generate three to six strategically different candidates from available
evidence:

- desired outcome;
- painful alternative displaced;
- specific audience and situation;
- differentiated mechanism;
- proof-led result;
- fit, service, or risk advantage;
- familiar category bridge for a novel offer.

Strategic candidates use different reasons to choose. “Close the books faster”
and “Finish month-end sooner” are wording variants, not separate strategies.
Stop when every supportable angle has one candidate and new entries only
paraphrase an existing angle.

### Apply hard gates

Reject any candidate that is not:

- true and supportable;
- desirable to One Reader;
- meaningfully distinct from the actual alternative;
- specific enough to compare;
- compatible with the category frame and fit boundary;
- focused on one dominant value.

Only survivors are scored for:

- immediate clarity;
- audience relevance;
- credible specificity;
- succinctness without lost meaning;
- distinct gist;
- adaptability across required channels.

A familiar-comparison formula can explain a novel offer, but keep it out of the
final proposition when it makes the product sound derivative. “Only,” “best,”
“fastest,” and quantified outcomes require substantiation. Actual recall and
market preference belong to validation; this procedure can only check whether a
candidate offers a distinct gist worth testing.

### Record the decision

Keep:

| Candidate | Strategic angle | Evidence | Gate result | Score | Decision reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Preserve rejected candidates and reasons. This prevents future wording debates
from reopening settled strategy without new evidence.

## Build a required-belief hierarchy

Treat the hierarchy as a belief inventory, not a fixed six-section sequence.
For each route, consider:

1. **Relevance:** This applies to my situation.
2. **Problem or want:** The issue or progress is important now.
3. **Value:** The outcome is worth attention or change.
4. **Category or approach:** I understand the kind of solution.
5. **Differentiation:** This approach fits better than my actual alternative.
6. **Belief:** The mechanism and proof make the claim credible.
7. **Fit and risk:** Important objections and limitations have honest answers.
8. **Offer:** I understand what I receive and under which terms.
9. **Action:** The next decision is clear and worth its cost.

Mark each belief:

- **already held:** entry evidence supports it;
- **must establish:** required before the next decision;
- **support if questioned:** optional depth for objections or comparison;
- **not applicable:** irrelevant to this route.

Start with the first unresolved belief appropriate to awareness. A product-aware
route should not repeat category education. A problem-aware route should not
assume solution familiarity. Channel guidance decides the physical order and
expression.

Use a message ledger:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Message ID | stable governance reference |
| Route | reader and entry context |
| Reader question | decision question answered |
| Belief status | held, establish, support, or N/A |
| Approved meaning | canonical claim or idea, not polished copy |
| Substantiation | source that supports it |
| Prerequisite | belief required first |
| Next belief or decision | intended progression |
| Guardrail | qualifier or prohibited overstatement |
| Priority | primary, supporting, optional |

Combine duplicate messages that answer the same question with the same proof.
Split one message when it tries to carry two different claims or values.

## Route multiple audiences

| Audience relationship | Architecture |
|---|---|
| Same job, value, alternative, awareness, and decision | one route and common core |
| Same position; examples or proof differ | common core with audience support modules |
| Same position; awareness differs | separate lead routes that converge on common core |
| Same buying decision; user and buyer differ | one primary reader with stakeholder support |
| Different job, value, category frame, or decision | separate messaging routes |
| Core promises or objections conflict | separate positioning tracks |
| Differences are unknown | mark unresolved; do not average |

Every route gets one One Reader, awareness diagnosis, dominant value, lead
requirement, unresolved-belief inventory, and next decision. Stop adding routes
when a new audience changes only wording, examples, or proof emphasis.

## Produce the messaging governance package

Keep one compact document unless project conventions require separate files.

### Decision header

Record scope, owner, version, date, status, accepted evidence set, and review
trigger. Useful statuses are:

- `draft`;
- `conditional`;
- `approved for downstream drafting`;
- `deprecated`.

“Approved” means internally accepted for drafting. It does not mean the market
validated the message.

### Core strategy card

Record:

- One Reader and situation;
- actual alternative;
- category frame;
- positioning statement;
- dominant value;
- chosen value proposition;
- mechanism or reason to believe;
- fit boundary and tradeoff.

### Route cards

For each route record awareness, familiarity, lead choice, already-held beliefs,
required beliefs, primary objection, proof needs, and next decision.

### Claim and guardrail ledger

| Claim | Evidence | Scope/qualifier | Prohibited overstatement | Owner | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

No approved claim should lack substantiation, scope, and an owner. Keep weak
claims conditional or as `[PLACEHOLDER: evidence needed]`.

### Terminology controls

Record approved category terms, preferred value language, customer phrases that
retain useful meaning, ambiguous terms that need definition, and generic claims
to avoid. Do not freeze voice or channel phrasing in the strategy; govern meaning
and factual boundaries.

### Decision log and review triggers

Record why the segment, category, position, value, proposition, or route changed.
Review when new evidence changes:

- target segment or commercial priority;
- product capability, offer, price, or limitation;
- available proof;
- actual alternatives or category language;
- competitor sameness;
- traffic source or route awareness.

## Stopping rules

| Stage | Stop when | If not satisfied |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | key decisions have evidence or explicit assumptions | keep draft; request resolving evidence |
| Segmentation | remaining differences do not change the decision case | merge them |
| One Reader | one candidate passes all gates and priority is clear | require owner choice or separate route |
| Category | lowest-complexity truthful frame orients the reader | do not coin a category |
| Differentiation | one desirable, defensible difference survives | report an offer or evidence gap |
| Awareness | each route has a state and confidence label | mark assumed or split the route |
| Sophistication | lowest credible response breaks observed sameness | do not escalate the promise |
| Value proposition | new candidates only paraphrase covered angles | select or record unresolved decision |
| Hierarchy | every required question has one primary message and proof | remove duplication or fill the gap |
| Routing | a new audience changes no lead, value, frame, or decision | use a support module |
| Governance | approved claims have evidence, scope, and owner | keep them conditional |

## Acceptance checklist

The strategy is ready for downstream drafting only when:

- [ ] Each route has one actor in one specific decision situation.
- [ ] The actual alternative includes workarounds and status quo where relevant.
- [ ] One evidence-backed value outranks the others for each route.
- [ ] The category helps readers place the product against familiar options.
- [ ] Positioning names one desirable and defensible difference.
- [ ] A competitor cannot substitute its name without making the position
      materially false.
- [ ] Fit boundaries and meaningful tradeoffs are explicit.
- [ ] Awareness is diagnosed per route and labeled observed, inferred, or
      assumed.
- [ ] Sophistication responds to observed market sameness rather than assumed
      maturity.
- [ ] The value proposition passes truth, desirability, distinction,
      specificity, category, and dominant-value gates.
- [ ] The hierarchy covers unresolved beliefs instead of imposing one universal
      order.
- [ ] Conflicting audiences have separate routes or positioning tracks.
- [ ] Every approved claim has substantiation, scope, owner, and guardrail.
- [ ] Canonical strategy, route adaptations, and final channel copy remain
      distinct.
