# Persuasion and Copy Frameworks

Frameworks organize an evidence-backed argument. They do not supply the insight,
claim, proof, or urgency. Reach for a framework only after the reader, dominant
value, positioning, and awareness are decided; frameworks then choose the
argument's shape and local mechanics.

## Scope and handoffs

This capsule decides structure, headline mechanics, body-copy units, proof
placement, objection treatment, and CTA craft. `messaging-positioning.md` owns
positioning, awareness, and the belief inventory. `pages.md` owns page sections
and asset layout. `email.md` owns sequences. This capsule does not pick the
position or the page plan; it picks the persuasive move.

Load this capsule when the task involves a named formula (AIDA, PAS), headline
construction, body-copy paragraphs, proof blocks, objection rebuttals, or CTA
wording. Load `messaging-positioning.md` first if awareness, dominant value, or
hierarchy is unresolved.

## Formula selection

Formulas are not steps you must complete. Each one answers a specific belief
deficit.

| Reader's missing belief | Structure | Why it works | Reject when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reader does not feel the problem yet | PAS: problem, consequences, solution | raises problem salience before the offer | pain is obvious, or agitation becomes manipulative |
| Reader needs one complete short argument | AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action | checks progression to a next step | it forces unrelated sections or replaces message hierarchy |
| Reader compares categories | BAB: before, after, bridge | contrasts present state with a future state | the before/after glosses over how much work the change takes |
| Reader knows the product, not the category | product-led opening | matches the entry state | you skip the want or problem when the reader is product-aware |
| Reader wants proof before engagement | Pillars: claim, reason, demonstration | carries a heavier argument with less narrative | proof is decorative (logos without results) |
| Feature-heavy copy lacks relevance | Feature → consequence → valued outcome → proof | connects mechanism to progress | the outcome or proof is invented |
| Reader is comparing alternatives | Current way → cost/limit → new mechanism → contrast → proof | makes differentiation concrete | competitor claims are guessed or distorted |
| Reader needs a relatable decision | Situation → struggle → choice → outcome | story gives context and identification | the brand becomes the hero or facts are embellished |

Choose the smallest argument that closes the belief gap. Not every page needs a
formula; many need only a clear assertion, relevance, and proof. If two formulas
fit equally, prefer the one with the least invented content.

## Headlines

A headline must first orient and match intent. Score it on:

1. expectation match with traffic or context;
2. immediate clarity;
3. the specific value or problem it names;
4. pull to continue;
5. honest differentiation.

The headline gives the reader a reason to keep reading, not the reward itself.
“How to [accomplish task] without [pain]” beats an unexplained claim. The
subhead can carry who, how, or proof; do not cram every detail into the H1.

Rapid-generation frames (one at a time, then edit):

- “How to [outcome] without [pain]”
- “[Outcome] in [time frame]”
- “[Specific audience]: your [task] just got [easier/faster]”
- “Why [common approach] fails [specific audience]”
- “The [noun] that [unexpected result]”

Reject headlines that only name the page, praise the company, use a claim every
competitor can make, bury the point under cute language, or promise a benefit
the page cannot deliver. The reader, not the writer, decides whether a headline
is clever.

## Body-copy units: assertion, why, proof

Convert features into a moving argument:

- **Assert:** the feature, fact, or claim as a point.
- **Connect:** why it matters to this reader: consequence or outcome.
- **Prove:** evidence or demonstration that makes the assertion true.

The three together move one belief. Stop when the claim is closed; do not
repeat it in a new way. Larger claims need larger proof: quantify or name the
before/after state, not “big savings” but “drops your forecast prep from 3 hours
to 25 minutes per week.”

For an ordinary feature, one why and one proof are enough. For a competitive or
high-risk claim, add a demonstration or a named case, not a louder adjective.

## Proof blocks

| Claim type | Proof that fits | Proof that misses |
|---|---|---|
| Performance result | quantified, dated result, named segment | logo wall, vague “thousands” |
| Capability | product demonstration, docs | marketing-style assertion |
| Fit (“for people like me”) | specific customer story | a testimonial about a different use case |
| Trust | authority, policy, guarantee with terms | the same generic quote stacked twice |
| Urgency | real deadline or constraint | artificial countdown |

One proof can establish one claim. If the proof names a case that does not match
the segment, it moves the claim sideways.

A testimonial has a job: state a result, name a specific barrier it overcame, or
answer an objection. Do not use one until it serves that job.

## Objections

List objections by severity and frequency. Place answers where the reader's own
mind raises the objection, not in fine print the reader must hunt for.

| Objection type | Answer move |
|---|---|
| Factual doubt | named mechanism, limitation, or independent evidence |
| Risk | policy, guarantee with terms, precedent, transparent limitation |
| Effort | process proof, concrete demonstration, shorter setup path |
| Trust | relevant authority, named customer, verified proof |
| Fit | honest qualification, an intended-user story |

Do not rebut a legitimate limitation with false confidence. Name it and show the
boundary. When the objection is real but rare, qualify: “For teams that also
need X, the upgrade handles it.”

Over-arguing an objection loses credibility. The goal is that the reader stops
doubting, not that the copy wins a fight.

## Friction words: the reader's own resistance

Friction words make the reader feel the work of the action before they choose
it. Inspect “Buy”, “Sign up”, “Submit”, “Join”, “Learn”, and “Start” for
collision with the reader's uncertainty.

- Name the result, not the action: “Download the roadmap” outperforms “Get
  started” when a free resource is the ask.
- Keep truth: never swap “Submit your payment” for “Continue” and hide the
  charge.
- Use “Now” or “Instantly” only when true.
- If the step is checkout, the reader should see “Pay”. If that hurts, the copy
  above it has failed, not the button.

Text links converting better than buttons is a real outcome worth testing for
low-commitment asks. Buttons still work for purchase, signup, and other
commitment actions. Test, do not assume.

## CTAs and the “I want to” test

For each CTA, apply RAD:

- **Require:** does the reader have enough information to accept the
  commitment?
- **Acquire:** is the action findable and complete without friction?
- **Desire:** does the wording carry the value of the next step?

Name the next step honestly and specifically. “See my dashboard” passes the
“I want to see my dashboard” test; “Click here” fails it.

Then one semantic CTA per page or email; repeat it where a reader could
rationally act. Never stack a second competing action at the same reader
moment. On long pages, place a CTA after each block that completes a belief,
not at fixed intervals.

Real urgency is a date, capacity, policy, or inventory constraint. Invented
urgency (“only 2 left”, “offer expires tonight”) breaks trust and is excluded
from this skill.

## Reluctant-hero copy

A reluctant hero is the reader who wants the outcome but fears the step: the
small-firm owner who wants new clients but hates “marketing”. The pattern:

1. Start where the reader is: current state, habit, reluctant self-image.
2. Name the want and the hesitation.
3. Let the reader act through the copy (customer is the hero; product is the
   guide).
4. Give a step small enough to take.

The reader's identity, pride, and fear move, not the headline's. When a claim
would smear or shame the reader to look stronger, it is dishonest and rejected.

## When frameworks fail

- The formula becomes a costume and the argument disappears.
- The framework forces a section the decision does not need.
- Assumed urgency replaces evidence.
- The copy speaks to the company's yes, not the reader's next step.
- The framing is wrong before any test runs: testing cannot rescue a message
  the reader has no reason to believe.

## Acceptance checks

- The structure is the smallest argument for the belief gap.
- Every formula is a tool, not a justification for its own content.
- Each body unit closes with reason plus proof, never a feature label alone.
- Proof appears beside its claim, at the scale the claim needs.
- Objections are answered where they begin; no bait and switch.
- The CTA passes Require/Acquire/Desire and the “I want to” test.
- No invented urgency, testimonials, numbers, or capabilities.
- No generic template could replace this copy without losing something
  specific.
