# Mechanism Cards: From Psychology to Predictions

Use a card only after recording an observed task break. A useful mechanism predicts what should change, what should not change, and what evidence would defeat it. If two cards make the same prediction, the test cannot distinguish them.

## Card template

```text
Observation (no theory):
Context and affected segment:
Candidate mechanism:
Why it fits:
Competing explanation, including a technical cause:
Predicted behavior if true:
Anti-prediction / result that weakens it:
Smallest coherent intervention:
Discriminating probe:
Immediate outcome and delayed guardrails:
Confidence and source boundary:
```

## Attention and perceptual organization

**Fits:** an available cue, state change, relationship, or action is not noticed while attention is plausibly elsewhere.

- Predict: moving feedback near the current focus or clarifying grouping improves noticing without teaching.
- Weakens: users notice the element but misunderstand it; then comprehension or information scent is more plausible.
- Test: first action, unprompted notice, grouping explanation, or same-task comparison. Do not infer attention from gaze alone: fixation does not prove interpretation.
- Interventions: proximity, common region, restrained salience, stable placement, persistent status, and redundant noncolor cues.

## Memory and learning

**Fits:** users must carry values, requirements, locations, or prior decisions across time or views.

- Predict: visible references, preserved values, meaningful chunks, or stable placement reduce revisits and memory-dependent errors.
- Weakens: information is visible and correctly recalled, but users still cannot decide; choice or trust may dominate.
- Test: compare errors and backtracking; ask for delayed recall only when remembrance is itself the product outcome.
- Interventions: recognition over recall, external memory, comparison views, resumability, and consistency. Chunking organizes meaning; it is not a license to force arbitrary item counts.

## Mental models and information foraging

**Fits:** first clicks, labels, destinations, or expected system behavior diverge from what the interface does.

- Predict: familiar vocabulary, stronger destination cues, and visible consequences improve first-path accuracy.
- Weakens: users predict the destination correctly but avoid it because effort, risk, or value is unfavorable.
- Test: ask where they would go and what they expect before interaction; compare path, success, and explanation.
- Interventions: task-language labels, explicit destination/consequence, orientation, and lower within- and between-patch search cost.

## Choice and judgment

**Fits:** people cannot narrow, compare, or commit among options, especially under uncertainty or consequence.

- Predict: support matched to decision strategy improves correctness and confidence: filters for eliminating many alternatives, comparison for weighing a few.
- Weakens: users understand differences but distrust terms or cannot afford the commitment.
- Test: option teach-back, decision accuracy, reversal/regret, and downstream fit—not speed alone.
- Interventions: remove irrelevant decisions, expose material differences, provide transparent suitable defaults, preserve autonomy, and make reversal proportionate.

Loss aversion, anchoring, framing, scarcity, authority, and social proof describe influences, not permission to manipulate. Material costs, uncertainty, sponsorship, defaults, and urgency must remain truthful and comprehensible.

## Motor effort and interaction cost

**Fits:** the intended action is understood but slow, error-prone, distant, small, repetitive, or physically demanding.

- Predict: larger/nearer targets, fewer transfers, and safer separation reduce slips across relevant input modes.
- Weakens: target activation is accurate but users choose the wrong action; semantics or hierarchy is the issue.
- Test: misactivation, correction, task completion, and keyboard/touch/switch/voice paths where relevant.
- Interventions: target geometry, distance, tolerance, control separation, semantic input types, and elimination of needless actions. Fitts’s law applies to target acquisition, not all hesitation.

## Motivation, autonomy, and wellbeing

**Fits:** users can act but perceived value, competence, autonomy, relatedness, or energy does not justify effort.

- Predict: clearer value, useful progress, meaningful control, and easier resumption improve intended voluntary completion.
- Weakens: forced completion or reminder pressure moves the metric while regret, disablement, or complaints worsen.
- Test: intended return, confidence, completion quality, exit control, regret, and delayed wellbeing—not raw engagement.
- Interventions: remove sludge, preserve choices, explain progress, support stopping and resumption, and avoid vortex-producing detours.

## Trust, persuasion, and commitment

**Fits:** requested money, data, permanence, or reliance exceeds established credibility and control.

- Predict: progressive commitment, truthful disclosure, verifiable evidence, and easy decline/reversal improve comprehension and durable trust.
- Weakens: users understand and trust the request but still find it irrelevant or unaffordable.
- Test: teach-back of terms and system limits, voluntary choice, cancellation success, refunds, complaints, and trust after use.
- Interventions: disclose material facts before commitment, match proof to claim, label automation limits, and make cancellation comparable to signup.

## Emotion, aesthetics, and remembered experience

**Fits:** first impression, peak failure, ending, or visual quality changes confidence or memory beyond functional performance.

- Predict: after utility and recovery work, coherent visual treatment or a better peak/end improves confidence and later recall without harming completion.
- Weakens: preference rises while errors persist—the aesthetic-usability halo may be masking defects.
- Test: task success plus confidence, unprompted interpretation, and delayed recall. Separate visceral preference from behavioral and reflective value.

## Practitioner-bias card

Apply this card to the team on every loop:

- What result would make us abandon the favored diagnosis?
- Which evidence was excluded, and why?
- Are we generalizing from ourselves, one vivid quote, or a convenient sample?
- Did framing, anchoring, halo, functional fixedness, or confirmation bias shape the options?
- Can a reviewer reconstruct observation → alternatives → prediction → decision from the ledger?
