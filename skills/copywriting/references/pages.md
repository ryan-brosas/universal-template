# Pages, Ads, Onboarding, and UX Copy

Load this capsule for web pages, ads, onboarding, and conversion microcopy. Load
messaging first when positioning, audience, awareness, or hierarchy is unsettled.
This capsule decides where messages appear, what evidence sits beside them, and
when the next action has been earned.

## Scope and handoffs

Inputs from messaging should include the primary reader, arrival state, dominant
value, differentiator, objections, approved claims, and proof inventory. If one
of those is missing, label the assumption instead of solving it with fluent
copy.

Keep continuity across touchpoints:

```text
source promise or query
→ page echo and qualifier
→ decision case and proof
→ CTA expectation
→ post-click state
```

A page can fail even when each sentence is good if one handoff changes the
promise, audience, offer, or required commitment.

## Page decision brief

Before drafting, record:

- **Arrival:** source, query or promise, awareness, and what the reader expects.
- **Reader:** primary segment, triggering situation, desired progress, and
  current alternative.
- **Offer:** what is included, mechanism, terms, limits, and meaningful contrast.
- **Action:** one primary conversion event, next state, and perceived cost in
  money, time, effort, data, and risk.
- **Belief gaps:** what must become true or credible before the action is
  sensible.
- **Evidence:** demonstrations, product facts, policies, testimonials, results,
  and prohibited claims.
- **Measurement:** nearest behavior the changed copy can affect and a downstream
  guardrail.

For a rewrite, name the observed failure before changing prose. “The copy feels
weak” is not a diagnosis. Prefer “problem-aware paid visitors continue to the
pricing section but do not select a plan; interviews show uncertainty about
which plan fits.” Send experiment design to `editing-validation.md`.

## Shared conversion gates

### 1. Arrival and hero gate

Treat the eyebrow, H1, subhead, primary CTA, and nearby proof as one orientation
unit. A reader should quickly understand:

1. Am I in the right place?
2. What is this?
3. Is it for someone in my situation?
4. What valuable change does it offer?
5. Why should I believe or continue?
6. What is the next step?

Approve the hero only when it:

- continues the source promise or query instead of resetting the conversation;
- reaches the reader's point before the company's self-description;
- names a concrete problem, outcome, audience, or mechanism;
- remains immediately understandable without category insider knowledge;
- earns attention without unsupported novelty, hype, or superlatives;
- leaves qualification, mechanism, or proof to the subhead when forcing every
  detail into the H1 would reduce clarity.

A pricing page may use an eyebrow such as “Plans and pricing” for orientation and
a value-led H1. Assume every important page can be a direct or repeat entry;
do not require homepage context to understand its hero.

### 2. Claim-closure gate

For each decision-driving feature, fact, or claim:

1. **Assert:** state what is true.
2. **Connect:** answer “So what changes for this reader?”
3. **Prove:** attach evidence that establishes that exact assertion.
4. **Qualify:** state the relevant segment, condition, plan, period, or limit.
5. **Close:** stop when the reader question is answered and sufficiently proved.

Increase proof with the claim's novelty, magnitude, comparativeness, price, and
risk. One concrete consequence and one matched proof point may close an ordinary
claim. Add copy only for another belief gap, objection, or proof burden.

Do not use:

- logos as proof of a performance claim;
- decorative screenshots as proof of a workflow claim;
- one customer's result as the expected result;
- a feature label as its own relevance argument;
- repeated assertions where the page needs mechanism or evidence.

A useful product demonstration shows:

```text
starting state → user action → product response → observable outcome
```

### 3. Section and length gate

Give each section a decision job. Common jobs are orientation, problem
recognition, outcome, mechanism, differentiation, proof, fit, objection,
risk reduction, offer, and action.

For each proposed section ask:

- Which reader question does it answer?
- Which new belief or evidence does it add?
- Why does it belong here rather than earlier or later?
- What should the reader be ready to consider next?

Remove sections that restate a closed claim. Merge proof into the claim it
supports. Move major objections into the argument where they arise; reserve a
final FAQ for secondary questions.

Page length follows the work required to understand, believe, and accept the
offer. Familiar low-risk actions may need little copy. Novel, expensive,
high-risk, or multi-stakeholder decisions need more mechanism, proof, terms, and
objection handling. Cut repetition before cutting evidence.

### 4. Action-readiness gate

Every primary action must pass RAD:

- **Require:** Has the reader received enough information for the requested
  commitment?
- **Acquire:** Is the control easy to find in the normal reading path and
  visually dominant over secondary actions?
- **Desire:** Does the label name a familiar next step and its immediate value?

The information required rises with unfamiliarity and with the money, time,
data, effort, or risk requested. Place a CTA where a reader could rationally
act, not at a prescribed fold or interval. An early CTA is valid for a familiar,
low-cost action. Otherwise build value first or offer an honest lower-commitment
step.

Review labels such as “Buy,” “Sign up,” “Submit,” “Complete,” “Join,” and
“Learn” for perceived cost and specificity. They are review flags, not banned
words. Never soften accurate commitment language in a way that hides payment,
renewal, data use, effort, or irreversibility. Use “Now,” “Instantly,” “Free,” or
“No card” only when the full flow makes the statement true.

Repeat the same semantic action when useful. Do not introduce equally weighted
competing actions at one decision state. Copy should name findability and visual
priority as design requirements; it cannot guarantee them.

## Asset-specific decisions

### Landing page

**Job:** continue one acquisition promise to one action for one qualified
visitor state.

Build the page from a message-match row:

| Handoff | Decision |
|---|---|
| Source | exact promise, query, creative, or audience qualifier |
| Hero | recognizable echo plus sharper value |
| Body | mechanism and proof for the source claim |
| Objections | barriers specific to this traffic and offer |
| CTA | next state implied by the source and page |

Choose the lead from awareness:

- Problem-aware traffic: mirror the recognized problem and desired progress,
  then introduce the solution.
- Solution-aware traffic: differentiate the approach and mechanism.
- Product-aware traffic: lead with fit, proof, objection resolution, or offer.
- Most-aware traffic: make terms and action prominent; do not force category
  education.

Remove navigation and unrelated exits when they compete with a campaign's one
conversion job. Keep necessary legal, accessibility, privacy, and support paths.
A long landing page should offer a concise orientation path plus deeper proof
and objection handling, not two unrelated arguments.

**Acceptance:** source, hero, proof, offer, CTA, and post-click state form one
uninterrupted proposition. Every destination claim is supportable from the page.

### Homepage

**Job:** establish the company's position, orient qualified visitors, and route
them without becoming “everything for everyone.”

Use a common core:

1. category and primary value;
2. strongest audience or use-case recognition;
3. differentiated mechanism;
4. relevant proof;
5. routes by audience, task, product, or lifecycle stage;
6. dominant company-level action.

For every route specify:

```text
audience or task → destination outcome → distinct link label → destination
```

Use shared core copy only where audiences share the same job and decision case.
Branch when their triggers, values, objections, or offers differ. One route
should remain visually and verbally primary. Avoid a hero carousel that asks the
visitor to assemble the position from rotating messages.

**Acceptance:** a new visitor can name the category and value; returning or
specialized visitors can identify their path; route labels predict their
destination; the primary path remains clear.

### Product or feature page

**Job:** turn capability into valued, believable use.

Organize around a user situation rather than an internal feature taxonomy:

```text
use case → current friction → capability/mechanism → demonstrated workflow
→ observable consequence → proof or limitation → CTA
```

For each capability, show what initiates it, what the user does, what the product
does, and what changes afterward. Use annotated screenshots, short demos, sample
outputs, or product documentation when they prove the claim. State plan,
integration, role, volume, or setup limits near the capability they constrain.

Do not claim an outcome merely because the feature could theoretically enable
it. Distinguish capability proof (“the export includes these fields”) from
performance proof (“teams reduced reconciliation time by X”).

**Acceptance:** no important feature appears as a label-only card. Each has a
reader consequence and matched demonstration, evidence, or marked placeholder.

### Pricing page

**Job:** reinforce why the product is worth choosing and make plan selection
comprehensible.

#### Standalone-entry check

The hero should orient readers who arrive directly, reconnect value for repeat
visitors, and preserve access to the actual prices. Do not replace clear
wayfinding with a cryptic value headline or hide pricing behind persuasion.

#### Plan-selection diagnosis

Before rewriting cards, determine what qualifies a customer for each plan:

- usage, seats, volume, storage, or capacity;
- capabilities or workflow complexity;
- service and support;
- security, control, compliance, or procurement;
- a documented hybrid.

Put the intended audience and selection criterion before a long feature list.
If plans differ by usage, make units and overages explicit. If they differ by
capability, explain which decision or workflow each capability unlocks.

#### Choice-architecture pass

Audit:

1. plan names and fit statements;
2. price, billing period, taxes, currency, contract, and renewal;
3. units, limits, overages, exclusions, and “unlimited” conditions;
4. support, migration, implementation, security, and procurement;
5. CTA wording and resulting state for each plan;
6. plan order and visual emphasis;
7. rationale for any “recommended” plan;
8. custom, enterprise, or contact-sales path.

Spell out ambiguous symbols and compare like with like. Explain why a plan is
recommended rather than using an unexplained badge. If recommendation depends
on customer state, let the fit statement do the work instead of manufacturing a
universal winner.

Treat expensive-first order, option count, anchors, annual toggles, and hidden
enterprise tiers as hypotheses. A high-priced option may repel, orient, signal
robustness, or show a growth path. The correct interpretation depends on the
buyer's selection logic and observed behavior.

#### Measurement handoff

Track each plan CTA and custom path separately. Name the nearest behavior the
changed lever can affect. A table change can support a conclusion about plan
progression; it cannot support a revenue conclusion unless completed purchase
or revenue was measured. Record every changed element. A multi-change winner
validates that bundle in that context, not each ingredient.

**Acceptance:** every card answers “for whom?”, “chosen by what criterion?”,
“what is included and limited?”, “what support applies?”, “what will I pay?”,
and “what happens next?”

### Sales page

**Job:** carry a higher-consideration decision to a complete, credible offer.

Map required belief clusters before choosing a formula:

1. relevance and stakes;
2. desired outcome;
3. approach and mechanism;
4. why this offer and why not the current alternative;
5. deliverables, process, and fit;
6. proof proportional to the promise;
7. material objections and legitimate limitations;
8. price, terms, risk, and next step.

Repeat the same CTA only at genuine readiness points: after a reader has enough
information to accept the offer, not after arbitrary blocks. Put price,
guarantee, cancellation, eligibility, deadline, and fulfillment terms near the
decision. Use urgency only when tied to a real date, capacity, inventory, or
policy.

**Acceptance:** every major objection from the brief has an answer before the
final CTA; exceptional results are qualified; terms do not rely on footnotes to
reverse the main promise.

### Ad

**Job:** earn a qualified next step while creating a promise the destination can
keep.

Use one audience cue, one problem or value, one necessary qualifier or proof
cue, and one predictable action. Select the angle from campaign evidence rather
than trying to summarize the whole product. If the platform fixes the CTA label,
clarify the destination state in the body copy.

Check the handoff both ways:

- Every ad claim appears or is proved on the destination.
- The landing hero echoes the ad's audience and promise.
- The CTA does not imply a lower commitment than the landing flow requires.
- Character limits do not remove a material condition.

**Acceptance:** the ad and destination could be read as adjacent sections of one
argument. Click-through is a local signal, not proof of qualified conversion.

### Onboarding

**Job:** move the user from the current state to the first observable value and
then to the next useful behavior.

Define:

```text
starting state → first-value event → smallest reversible action
→ product response → success feedback → next action
```

Give each state one primary task. Explain why permissions, integrations, data,
or setup work are needed at the point of request. State whether the user can
skip, save, resume, undo, or return later. Use progressive disclosure when the
first-value event does not require full configuration.

Copy by state:

- **Orientation:** confirm where the user is and the immediate goal.
- **Instruction:** name the user action and necessary constraint, not every UI
  detail.
- **Progress:** show what is complete and what remains when that knowledge helps.
- **Success:** name what changed and offer the next useful action.
- **Interruption:** preserve work, explain recovery, and avoid blame.
- **Permission:** explain purpose, scope, consequence, and alternative.

Do not promise a value moment the product cannot yet deliver or use celebration
to hide incomplete setup.

**Acceptance:** the first-value event is explicit; each state has one task,
feedback, and next action; setup costs and permissions are disclosed before the
user incurs them.

### UX and conversion microcopy

**Job:** make the current state, consequence, and available action understandable
without obscuring user choice.

Inventory relevant states:

- default and first use;
- loading and long-running work;
- empty and zero-result;
- success and partial success;
- validation error, system error, offline, and timeout;
- permission and privacy request;
- warning and destructive action;
- cancellation, downgrade, expiry, and renewal.

Use these state patterns:

| State | Required copy |
|---|---|
| Empty | value of filling the state + first useful action |
| Loading | what is happening + whether the user can leave or cancel |
| Error | what happened + impact + what remains safe + recovery |
| Partial success | what completed + what failed + next options |
| Success | what changed + relevant next action |
| Permission | purpose + data/scope + consequence of allow/deny |
| Destructive | named object + consequence + reversibility + exact confirmation |

Buttons and links should predict the immediate result. Labels should distinguish
save, publish, send, pay, subscribe, delete, remove, archive, and cancel rather
than collapsing materially different actions into “Continue.” Confirmations
should not make the unsafe action easier to choose by accident. Never use
microcopy to obscure subscriptions, defaults, data use, cancellation, or
irreversible consequences.

**Acceptance:** every relevant state has copy; errors support recovery;
destructive confirmations name the object and consequence; the label matches
what the system does next.

## Page acceptance checklist

### Shared

- [ ] The brief names the originating promise and expected post-click state.
- [ ] The hero matches visitor motivation, is clear, and communicates concrete
      value without unsupported hype.
- [ ] Every decision-driving claim has a user consequence and matched proof or a
      visible proof placeholder.
- [ ] Larger claims carry proportionately stronger evidence and qualification.
- [ ] Every section closes a distinct reader question or adds necessary proof.
- [ ] Core objections appear where they arise, not only in a catch-all FAQ.
- [ ] Before each primary CTA, the page supplies the information required for
      its money, time, data, effort, and risk cost.
- [ ] CTA labels predict the immediate result and do not hide commitment.
- [ ] One action is dominant per decision state.
- [ ] The named metric is no broader than the changed page lever can support.

### Deliverable

Provide copy in page order. Add section-purpose labels when they clarify the
reasoning, but keep them out of publishable copy. Separate copy, design
requirements, instrumentation notes, and evidence requests. Mark screenshots,
integrations, prices, legal terms, guarantees, testimonials, and quantitative
proof that need verification as `[PLACEHOLDER: evidence needed]`.
